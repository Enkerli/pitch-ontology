# Where is the one?

The pitch side asks where the centre is. This is the same question in time, and it has
the same shape: a centre is established by what happens around it, not by its position
in a list.

Start with the assumption to be suspended. Dominant theory locates a cycle by its first
element — the downbeat — and a notation, a sequencer and a DJ mixer all require that
element before they can hold anything at all. The question is what other traditions put
in that place.

- **Sam** is an arrival. Improvisation aims at it and lands on it; the cycle is organized
  by approach. Translating it as "the downbeat" makes it a starting position and loses
  the aiming.
- **A gong stroke** in a Javanese gongan ends and begins the cycle at once. Asking which
  it is, is a question the notation created.
- **Clave** has no "one" of its own. It has a direction: which half comes first relative
  to the song and the other parts. Rotate the pattern and the notes are identical and the
  music is wrong.
- **A timeline** locates the parts against each other rather than against a count. It is
  an orientation device, and it does not need a first position to work.
- **A loop** in a sequencer has no first beat until something outside supplies one, which
  is why the same loop can be entered at several points and still be right. Here the
  ambiguity is a resource.
- **Four-on-the-floor** solves the problem by removing it — every beat is marked, so
  position is never in doubt. It does the work of a timeline by the opposite means, and
  unlike a timeline it survives rotation, because there is nothing asymmetric left to
  rotate.

- **An aksak cycle** has no even unit to be located against at all. Its units stand in a
  2:3 ratio, and Brăiloiu's argument in 1951 was that this is a system rather than a limp —
  the earliest refusal in this bibliography of the idea that unequal beats are deviations.

Now notice what the list is doing. Each of these is a way of making an ensemble locatable
in time, and only some of them are properties of a sequence. Clave direction, the gong
stroke and the timeline are relations — between parts, between a part and a dance, between
a stroke and an instrument. A representation that stores a rhythm as a string of onsets
holds none of them, because none of them is in the string.

That is the point where the Euclidean-rhythm literature becomes a case rather than a
result. Its identification of named patterns with maximally even sets is real, and the
recurrence across traditions is worth explaining. But the representation it works in is
invariant under rotation, and rotation is exactly the operation that destroys clave. The
finding and the loss come from the same choice.

Compare with `where-is-the-centre.md`. Both journeys end in the same place: a centre is
not an element, it is a relation that some element carries, and any model that stores
elements without relations will find the centre missing and conclude it was never there.
