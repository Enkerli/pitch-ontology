# What is a scale?

Treat SCALE first as an analytical operation: ordering pitch categories, often within
an octave.

Then ask what was removed:
- hierarchy
- trajectory
- phrase grammar
- register
- intonation variability
- instrument-specific tuning
- social or ritual function

Aka scale elicitation is a canonical stress test: a defensible analytical pitch model
need not correspond to a locally verbalized abstract object.

## A scale with no practitioners

The blues scale is the sharpest case available, because it inverts the usual problem.
Elsewhere the difficulty is that a locally named category resists being written as an
ordered pitch set. Here there is no locally named category at all: a six-note collection
was assembled by writers and teachers, and it now circulates through method books, jazz
curricula, guitar instruction and the scale menus of music software far more widely than
the recordings it claims to describe.

Run the same subtraction. What was removed is the thing the scale was invented to name.
The pitches at issue — the blue notes — are realized as variable regions, and a collection
stated on twelve equal-tempered degrees is exactly the notation that cannot hold them.

Then notice that the descriptions disagree, and that this is not a gap to be closed:

- Measurement of early recordings finds stable regions that do not sit on the grid.
- A corpus study of rock describes the same effect as alternation between two discrete
  pitches, which would put it back on the grid.
- An Africanist account derives these pitches from tonal systems built over a bourdon,
  in which nothing is lowered and nothing is blue.
- And a critical reading argues the object was produced by the act of describing blues
  through European theory in the first place.

The ontology's job is not to choose. It is to keep `blues_scale` and `blue_note` as
separate records, so that a claim about pedagogy is not mistaken for a claim about
what anyone sang.
